\begin{abstract}
Repeated probability surveys estimate a summary for each of many populations, and
those estimates are used to predict the corresponding summary for a population
that was not surveyed. The prediction must separate between-population variation
from sampling error, and the sampling error is itself estimated, from designs
with limited degrees of freedom. We ask what that costs.

The answer turns on a quantity this literature does not usually separate out:
the \emph{scale elasticity} of the interval, $\partial\log R/\partial\log\theta$,
where $R$ is the half-width and $\theta$ the latent scale nobody knows. What
separates competing constructions is not how well they estimate $\theta$---they
can be given the same estimate---but how hard the interval reacts to being wrong
about it. A band that deconvolves has elasticity $\rho^2/2$, damped by the design
share $\rho^2$; any band proportional to $\sqrt\theta$ has elasticity exactly
$1/2$. We give an exact exchange identity between the two, three consequences
that resolve otherwise puzzling reversals in which construction is narrower, and
a closed-form prediction of the width ratio that we register in advance and test
on a fresh grid, where it reproduces the dependence on the error budget to six
decimal places and its constant requires a located $1+1.9/K$ correction.

We then give a procedure that exploits the damping: an exact confidence set for a
relative design-variance structure, an exact pivot for the correction ratio, and
a conservative computational bound making the resulting interval an algorithm
rather than an optimisation. Under a restricted normal model it has a stated
guarantee. In finite-population complex-sample simulation we report both the
regimes where it delivers and those where it loses its intermediate guarantee.
On three pre-specified items from European Social Survey rounds 9--11 we report
what each construction needs as input and how far the intervals differ, under
stated approximations and with no coverage claim.
\end{abstract}
