Many survey methods subtract an estimate of sampling variance from the spread
observed across areas or populations, to recover how much those populations
truly differ. Small area estimation does this when it estimates a model
variance, and so do prediction methods that calibrate on survey-estimated
curves. We show that the two conditions such a procedure needs are not
independent. Subtraction leaves the standard error unchanged while shrinking
what it is divided by, so the design share that makes a correction worth
attempting is the same quantity that makes the remainder imprecise. The algebra
is classical; its consequence for deciding whether a correction may be used has
not been drawn out. The requirement is a closed-form boundary: the areas needed
grow as the square of the noise-to-signal ratio over the squared tolerance, so
holding a Fay--Herriot shrinkage weight stable to a tenth needs two hundred
areas at equal noise and signal, and 3,200 at four times the noise. We then
apply the boundary to a conformal procedure that gates on exactly these two
conditions and reports a fixed floor of 94 populations. That floor is the
boundary at a design share of zero, where nothing needs removing; where its own
need gate opens the requirement is 153. Across 45 configurations of European
Social Survey estimates, spanning five ways of defining the calibration unit,
the boundary predicts the gate correctly in 44 while the fixed floor does not.
Refining the unit reaches the correction only along a dimension on which the
domains genuinely differ: cutting by region comes within reach, cutting by sex
falls an order of magnitude short.
